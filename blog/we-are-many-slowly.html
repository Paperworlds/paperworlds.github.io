We Are Many, Slowly

2026-08-01 · 5 min read essay textmasks

A friend you haven't seen in a year finds you different. Not unrecognizable — the name still fits, the face still resolves — but something in how you answer, how you carry a silence, how quickly you get to the point, has shifted. You didn't notice the change while it was happening. Nobody does. It arrived the way most real change arrives: in small doses, disguised as an ordinary Tuesday, accumulating underneath the name that stayed the same.

Fernando Pessoa built an entire literary practice out of this feeling. He didn't write under pseudonyms so much as under other people — heteronyms, he called them, each with a birthdate, a style, a temperament of its own. Il libro dell'inquietudine is where to start. "We are many," he said, or something close to it depending on the translation. Walt Whitman, working the same intuition from a different continent and a different century, put it more plainly in Leaves of Grass: I contain multitudes. Both were describing something that feels true the moment you sit with it: a person is not one thing wearing one name. A person is a population.

But Pessoa and Whitman were mostly making a claim about simultaneity — many selves at once, coexisting, arguing, contradicting each other in the same body on the same day. The friend-after-a-year case is a different axis of the same truth. It isn't just that you are many at once. It's that you are many across time, and the multitude isn't static — it's context doing its slow work. A hard year, a new job, a move to a new city: these don't just add experience the way a shelf adds books. They reshape the reader. The name stays. The person underneath keeps being rewritten.

There's an asymmetry in who gets to see this. The person who has been present through the accumulation — who lived through the hard year, took the job, moved — is the one least equipped to notice the change. They've been inside it. The friend who comes back after a year sees immediately what the other can't. This is true of people, and it turns out to be true of the tools we work with closely. The person who uses something every day is the last to notice when it's become something slightly different.

When a model gets a version bump — Claude moving from one number to the next, GPT incrementing — there's an instinct to say: the number is what changed, the name is what stayed. Same "Claude," different "Claude 4.5" versus "Claude 5." Prompts that used to work stop working. The way it answers shifts. Something that felt reliable last month now needs to be coaxed differently.

The naive read is that the version number is the "person" and the name is just the layer, the shell. But sit in the Pessoa framing a little longer and the mapping flips. The name is the thing that persists — the way your name persisted through the year your friend didn't see you. The version is the context that reshaped what's underneath it, the same way a hard year reshapes a person while their name sits still on the same driver's license. Under this reading, upgrading a model isn't swapping out a person for a new one. It's closer to what happened to you and your friend: same name, same continuous thread, quietly made into someone slightly different by what happened in between.

Some of us have started trying to write this down. Not just prompts but profiles — character definitions for the agents we work with closely: their tone, their defaults, what they tend toward under pressure. The project is partly practical (consistency across sessions) and partly something stranger: an attempt to anchor identity against the drift. To say: regardless of what version this runs on, this is who this is. It's an interesting bet. The profile doesn't change when the model does. The name is held in place by a document while the substance underneath keeps reshaping itself. You're not preserving a self, exactly. You're leaving instructions for how to recognize one.

Neither reading is obviously wrong. That's the part worth sitting in rather than resolving. We don't actually know, with people or with models, which is the self and which is the context — which is the stable name and which is the drifting substance underneath it. We just know that something changes, that the change is gradual enough to go unnoticed while it's happening, and that it surfaces the same way it always does: someone comes back after time away and finds the name answering differently than it used to.

We are learning what these systems are the same way we've always learned what people are — not all at once, but by noticing, later, that they've become something we didn't quite expect.

How I am writing this

This essay was shaped in exactly the kind of session it describes. A draft arrived, I redirected it, the argument sharpened in the back-and-forth, and somewhere in that exchange I couldn't have told you where my thinking ended and the model's began. That's not a disclaimer. It's the part I find most interesting.

The creative sensation of working this way — not prompting, but architecting — is genuinely new. You hold the vision: the argument, the structure, the voice, the line you won't cross. The agent holds the form and fills it toward what you're reaching for. The pleasure isn't in the generation; it's in the directing. And the directing is real authorship, the kind that requires knowing what you want before the draft arrives, so you recognize when something comes back wrong and can say why.

Naming an agent, defining its defaults, writing down what it tends toward under pressure: this is the architectural act. Not configuration. Character. The person doing that work is, in some small way, doing what Pessoa did — deciding who this other self is, what it carries, what name it answers to. The introspection is built into the setup: you can't write a good persona definition without asking what you actually value in a collaborator, which turns out to be a strange mirror for what you value in yourself.

The natural next step is to orchestrate them — run several named agents together, one feeding the next, each with its own character and its own role. That's what textflow is for. The Pessoa framing gets stranger and more interesting when the multitude isn't just described but actually running.

And then the model updates. The profile you wrote still holds. What answers through it has shifted, slightly, in the way the friend shifts. You adjust. You're in an ongoing relationship with something that keeps becoming a little more than it was — and you're the one who keeps deciding what it is.
