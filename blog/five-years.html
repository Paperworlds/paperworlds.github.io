Five years

2026-07-06 · 5 min read essay

The company was five years old when I joined it, which felt like a sign at the time. Young enough to still have the speed of something being built, old enough to have architecture that could hold the weight. That window in a company's life is narrow — the point where you can still change the direction of a whole system in a morning, but something real already exists to change it toward. I caught that window.

Five years later, I left. The window had moved. They always do.

What crypto does to time is something you only notice from outside it. Markets run on no schedule that a normal week respects. Through the night, through the weekend, through the hour you planned to be somewhere else. You build reflexes around it — checking a price mid-sentence, not because it matters in that specific moment, but because the signal is always there and you trained yourself not to miss it. Over years that rhythm becomes yours. You stop noticing the shape of the days because the shape has become you.

The work itself was good. Interesting problems. A team that cared about the quality of the solution, not just that a solution existed. The kind of environment where someone stops you in the hall to explain why your implementation is subtly wrong, and you're grateful rather than defensive, because you know they're right. I stayed for five years because I wanted to. That's the only honest explanation for why anyone stays anywhere for that long.

The shift came quietly. Something in the family started to need more of me than the reflexes had room for. The math wasn't complicated once I let myself do it. I resigned. The reflex started to loosen almost immediately, which surprised me — how fast it went, once I stopped feeding it. Within a few weeks the pull of the signal was mostly gone. Five years of conditioning, and it dissolved in weeks. That taught me something about what those instincts are actually made of.

What you carry from five years at a place like that is harder to name than a skill set. The specific knowledge belongs to the room you were in. What travels is the layer underneath: how to reason when the answer matters and you won't get a second chance to run it. How to hold a bar without making the bar the only conversation. How to care about correctness in systems that can't fully stop. It's a second language now. It surfaces in things that have nothing to do with the original context.

The personal projects started before I left, honestly. Small things on weekends — tools for my own use, not because I planned to build a set of anything, but because I kept needing somewhere to put the thinking that doesn't fit in a backlog. After I resigned, the folder stopped being a weekend thing. Paperworlds is that folder, taken seriously. Not as a product, not yet. As a place to work on what I actually want to work on, at the pace that makes sense.

Five years leaves a mark. Not the kind that hurts — the kind that changes the shape of how you move through things. You gave the time, and in return you became someone who can do something you couldn't do before. The deal felt fair when I signed it, and it still feels fair from the other side. I don't know exactly what the next chapter holds. The direction is clearer than the destination, which is probably how it should be. That feels like enough to go on.
