Perfect tense

2026-07-30 · 5 min read reflections textforums
But what if it was simple?
And what if it made sense?
And what if we were living
In a perfect tense? Nothing But Thieves — Reset Me

The song asks it as a question because it doesn't know the answer yet. A wish dressed in a subjunctive — "what if we were living" — not we are, not we have been, but the hypothetical version of a state that doesn't exist but should. Simple. Making sense. Complete, in a way the present isn't. The perfect tense not as a memory or a fact, but as a condition you might arrive at if you were reset before things fell apart.

I found it on an electric scooter. No engine sound to sit inside — just wind and road and whatever your body does when it's moving faster than it's used to. I was sliding down a long curve and something shifted: I stopped piloting the scooter and started being carried by it, by the momentum and the lean and the road already written beneath me. I wasn't thinking about riding. I wasn't remembering having ridden. I was inside something that had already resolved, a motion that had already found its shape, and I was moving through the completed form of it. Like a wave that already knows where it's going, and you're just inside the knowing.

Grammar (how reality is being mapped)

The perfect tense is the one most people reach for wrong. It isn't a fancy past tense. It isn't a more finished version of "I rode." Grammatically, it marks an action that is completed but whose effect is still alive in the present. "I rode" is simple past — the action is over, sealed, a memory you're looking back at. "I have ridden" is perfect — the riding is finished, but something of it persists into now. The consequence is still warm. You're in the present and inside the aftermath of something completed at the same time, and the language is holding both at once.

What makes it strange is that double-exposure. The action is over. You're still in it. The song reaches for exactly this: "what if we were living in a perfect tense?" — living in the aftermath of something good, something complete, something that made sense and still does. Not anticipating it. Not remembering it. Inside it, now, with its consequence still running.

On the scooter, the wave already knew its shape. I was moving through the consequence of a motion still in progress, and the two things — the doing and the having done — collapsed into the same moment. That's what flow actually is, I think, when you try to describe it precisely rather than just calling it flow: the present tense and the perfect tense arriving together, the action completing itself while you're still inside it.

Digital (how reality can be perceived)

AI output arrives in the perfect tense. The work is done, handed over as a completed action whose process you never inhabited. You receive the consequence without the ride. This is genuinely new, and I'm not sure we've fully reckoned with how new it is: language, music, code have always come to us with a history behind them, even if invisible. You could feel the effort in the craft, imagine the process that made it, or at minimum know that a person spent present-tense time inside the work before it reached you. The perfect tense was always downstream of something. Now you can receive it without knowing what that something was, or whether there was a something at all.

The song asks "what if you reset me?" and AI can, in a real sense, do that. It can hand you the reset, the restart, the version of the thing before it fell apart. The result is real. The consequence persists. But the reset was performed by something that didn't feel the need for one — that has no present tense to speak of, no moment of being inside the motion. It produces the perfect tense without ever having lived the imperfect.

That's the strangest sentence I've written about AI: it produces the perfect tense without having lived the imperfect. The imperfect tense — the one that marks ongoing, incomplete, continuous action in the past — is the tense of the process. The struggle. The still-becoming. AI skips directly from nothing to consequence, and the consequence looks like ours.

Work (writer mode)

There is a mode of writing — any writer has found it and most will say it's the only time the work comes out right — where you are not deciding what comes next. You are listening. Something exists just beyond what you can see directly, already shaped, and you are following the sound of it, transcribing rather than composing, the sentence arriving before you chose it. The present tense of the sitting-and-writing and the perfect tense of the thing you're reaching for collapse into one act. You are on the wave. Characters do this too, when the internal logic is consistent enough that the writer stops specifying and starts discovering. Agents can feel that way as well — the thing generating itself from its own logic, the author shifting from generative to editorial.

Coding reaches that state too. It takes longer to get there and requires a different kind of surrender: stop specifying and start following what the thing wants to become. Creative instinct rather than design. The logic of the structure pulling you forward rather than you pushing it.

What I didn't expect is that vibe coding — the loop where you describe what you want and the AI drafts the shape of it — can stay close to that feeling, sometimes closer than writing alone does. Not because the AI is in flow. It isn't, in any meaningful sense. But it lowers the friction enough that your own flow has room to arrive. You sketch, it fills, you redirect, the thing takes shape, and somewhere in that exchange the work moves faster than your self-consciousness can interfere. The AI is part of the instrument. You are still inside the present tense.

Act (how a gift is used and given back)

On the scooter the perfect moment arrived because I had been fully inside the present tense first — and then outside of it. The wind and the road were happening, continuously, and the flow emerged from inside that happening — built up step by step until I wasn't in the steps anymore, I was in the shape they'd already made. The perfect tense was downstream of the present tense. One required the other. I couldn't have arrived at the wave without the riding that preceded it, and the wave wouldn't have felt like anything if I hadn't been on the scooter when it broke.

With AI you can skip to the consequence. The present tense — the drafting, the failing, the slow accumulation that normally earns the completion — isn't required. The result looks the same from the outside. The paragraph is there. The code runs. But you didn't arrive at it through the present tense. You received it. The perfect tense was handed over without the ride, which means it isn't quite the same tense, even if the grammar looks identical.

I don't think this means borrowed perfect tense is worthless — for plenty of things it's a genuine gift, a reset before collapse, exactly what the song was wishing for. But for the things that feel most worth doing, I keep coming back to the scooter: the wave that already knows its shape is beautiful to ride, and you can feel it because you were on the scooter when the shape arrived. I'm not sure you can feel it the same way from a photograph of the wave. The consequence is visible. The perfect is missing.
