Depth of collaboration

2026-09-01 · 5 min read essay

The Syntax survey (n=1,252) has a chart worth sitting with. It plots how much developers feel their skills are diminishing against how many AI agents they work with regularly. The expectation is a positive correlation: more agents, more delegation, more atrophy. The chart doesn't show that. The "skills diminishing" signal drops at four or five agents and keeps dropping. The people most embedded in AI-assisted work are the least worried about losing their skills.

The instinctive reading is that they've normalized the loss, or stopped caring, or lost the ability to notice. The more useful reading is that they've discovered something about how intensity of collaboration actually works — something that contradicts the fear, not because the fear is wrong about what atrophy is, but because it's wrong about what causes it.

What depth means

There are two ways to scale your use of AI. Horizontal: more projects, more tools, more agents, each covering more ground. Vertical: more loops on the same thing. Horizontal scales breadth. Vertical scales depth.

Each loop has a shape. You bring intention and judgment. The agent brings execution and surface area. Output comes back. You evaluate it, redirect it, correct it, push it further. The next prompt encodes something you learned from the last response, because the last response gave you something to react to that you didn't have before. Then the loop runs again.

Twenty tight loops on one project in a day is a different activity from two shallow loops on ten projects. Not just because more got done on the first project. Because something different happened in those twenty loops — each one required judgment that could only be exercised after seeing the previous output. That accumulation is what the embedded users in the survey found. The loops build something in the person running them, not just in the work.

Shallow use is where the atrophy actually lives

The shallow version of AI use looks like this: you delegate, you review, you accept or reject. The agent does the work; you assess the result. This is the version that erodes skill, not because the agent is doing too much, but because you're doing too little of the specific thing that develops judgment. Watching someone else work out is not a workout.

The distinction isn't about volume of AI use. It's about engagement density per loop. A shallow loop is one where your input to the next iteration doesn't depend meaningfully on your evaluation of the last output. You could have written the same prompt regardless of what came back. A deep loop is one where your redirect encodes something that required seeing the output first — a correction, a reframing, a push in a direction you didn't know existed until the agent surfaced it. The difference is whether you're directing or just reviewing.

This is why the survey finding runs counter to the intuition. The fear of skill atrophy assumes that more AI use means less exercise of the human skill. But intensity of engagement — many tight loops, each requiring active judgment — is itself the exercise. The embedded users aren't losing the skill because they're too deep in the loops to notice. They're not losing the skill because the loops are the skill.

The honest tax

None of this is free. Intensity costs something. Twenty tight loops on one project in a day leaves you tired in a specific way: you've been directing, evaluating, redirecting, for hours, and the fire that runs those loops is the human's. The agent doesn't provide it. The loops amplify it; they don't source it.

The no-weekends argument sits here. Ozio — rest, margin, the time when you're not directing anything — isn't the enemy of depth. It's the condition that makes depth sustainable. The fire needs refueling. An intensity that never lets up isn't depth; it's a different kind of shallow use, just with more volume. What distinguishes vertical scaling from overuse is whether the loops are accumulating judgment or just accumulating output.

The tax on intensity is honest in one important way: you feel it. You know when you've done twenty real loops on something because the tiredness at the end of the day is specific. That specificity is the signal that you were actually in the work, not just supervising it from the outside.

What the infrastructure needs to be

Depth of collaboration places a demand on the tooling that breadth doesn't. If each session starts fresh — blank context, no memory of what the loops built, no accumulated understanding — the depth resets. You're not deepening; you're starting over with better tools each time. The twenty loops from yesterday don't carry into today. The accumulation you're counting on isn't there.

This is what makes the memory question more than an ergonomics question. What an agent remembers between sessions shapes what the next loop can build on. Shallow memory means shallow loops regardless of how much time you spend or how tightly you engage. The infrastructure for depth isn't just fast inference or good tool coverage. It's reliable continuity: what was decided, what was learned, what direction was being pursued. Without that, vertical scaling isn't actually available. You can intend to go deep, but the system resets you to the surface.

The embedded users in the Syntax survey may be finding this instinctively. The ones running the most loops are also, almost certainly, the ones who have figured out how to carry context forward — through logs, through structured notes, through whatever scaffolding keeps the loops from restarting. Depth of collaboration is as much a practice of information architecture as it is a style of working.
